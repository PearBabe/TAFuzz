Abstract
As our dependence on the internet and digital platforms grows, the risk of cyber threats rises, making it essential to implement effective Measures to safeguard sensitive information through cybersecurity, ensure system integrity, and prevent unauthorized data access. Fuzz testing, commonly known as fuzzing, is a valuable for software testing as it uncovers vulnerabilities and defects in systems by introducing random data inputs, often leading to system crashes. In the Internet of Things domain, fuzzing is crucial for identifying vulnerabilities in networks, devices, and applications through automated tools that systematically inject malformed inputs into IoT systems. This research aims to comprehensively evaluate current fuzzing practices, emphasizing adaptive techniques tailored to IoT environments. A rigorous analysis of 30 recent academic articles was conducted to identify weaknesses, gaps, and challenges in existing approaches. The investigation revealed the need for novel fuzzing techniques that address rmware, hardware, and software vulnerabilities, as well as Denial of Service attacks in IoT systems. By exploring recent trends and identifying gaps and challenges, this research aims to advance IoT security, highlighting the need for improved fuzzing techniques and presenting future research directions to strengthen IoT cybersecurity.
I. INTRODUCTION
The Internet of Things (IoT) has become a paradigm that facilitates connectivity and data exchange among devices, sensors, and systems. It has profound implications for domains such as industrial automation, smart cities, healthcare, and transportation [1]. According to Yang et al. [2], the IoT could be one of the most important technologies of the twenty- rst century, as it offers chances for integrating the real world into computer systems. However, the complexity and diversity of IoT ecosystems pose notable security vulnerabilities and challenges. It is essential to address these challenges with innovative solutions to fully harness the potential of IoT.
1.1 Motivation
The Internet of Things (IoT) devices are equipped with various sensors, such as microphones, cameras, and thermometers, among others. These sensors continuously gather information about their surroundings, which may include personal and sensitive data. While smart devices offer features, convenience, and an improved lifestyle, they also result in the collection of a substantial amount of user data. This data is analyzed and subsequently transferred and stored across each of the IoT architecture layer [3], as illustrated in Figure 1. However, it is crucial to note that the IoT architecture’s layers are susceptible to various kinds of vulnerabilities, as demonstrated in Figure 2. To enhance their security, the vulnerabilities in IoT devices can be reduced before their deployment. However, this is not always possible.
Fuzzing is generally used to discover and address security vulnerabilities in systems, including during operation. [6]. According to Manes et al. [7], fuzzing remains famous because of its simplicity, ease of implementation, and the availability of extensive experimental data for uncovering real-world software vulnerabilities. Yet, its implementation in the IoT environment lags behind due to the distinctive features of IoT ecosystems, which include a diverse array of devices with varying functionalities, operating systems, and protocols. This renders traditional fuzzing techniques ineffective or impractical. [6]. Abla El Bekkali et al. stated that research related to cybersecurity in IoT, particularly regarding fuzzing, is in its early stages [3], characterized by different approaches and frameworks aimed at addressing the heterogeneity of IoT.
Existing studies primarily focus on adapting conventional fuzzing techniques to speci c IoT scenarios, which cannot be broadly applied across the diverse spectrum of IoT devices [6-211]. Specialized fuzzing techniques and tools tailored for embedded systems and IoT environments are essential, given that conventional security approaches may not adequately meet these unique challenges. Developing such techniques is crucial, as research on fuzzing for IoT cybersecurity is still in its early stages and characterized by different approaches aimed at addressing IoT's heterogeneity.
1.2 Aims and Objectives
This article seeks to advance fuzzing techniques for enhancing IoT security by systematically examining key contributions, highlighting challenges, evaluating the effectiveness and applicability of fuzzing methods in IoT contexts, identifying shortcomings and gaps in current approaches, and suggesting directions for future research. The objectives of this study are:
1. To perform an in-depth review of current fuzzing techniques, expand the existing knowledge on fuzzing, and describe the various methodologies, frameworks, and tools utilized to identify vulnerabilities in IoT devices and networks.
2. To explore and evaluate the effectiveness of existing fuzzing techniques in IoT systems.". It involves evaluation of the performance, scalability, and adaptability of these techniques in different IoT environments, considering factors such as resource constraints, device heterogeneity, and real-world applicability.
3. To compare and contrast different fuzzing approaches in order to highlight their strengths and weaknesses. By examining variations in methodologies, their implementation strategies, and outcomes, this study aims to offer a thorough grasp of how different fuzzing techniques perform in speci c scenarios, assessing their suitability for diverse IoT devices and applications.
4. To enhance IoT security through improved fuzzing techniques and strategies based on the literature review, the approach suggests developing automated tools, standardized benchmarks, and evaluation frameworks tailored to the speci c needs of IoT systems. These measures aim to improve the robustness and resilience of IoT devices against cyber threats.
5. To offer guidance for future research directions, inspiring innovation and the development of advanced, e cient fuzzing methods capable of addressing the distinct challenges posed by IoT ecosystems.
1.3 Limitations of Existing Fuzzing Techniques for IoT


 Page 3/30
Fuzzing involves repeatedly feeding malformed inputs into a target device or program, referred to as the System Under Test (SUT), to induce crashes and reveal potential vulnerabilities. It is a widely used and effective technique for detecting software aws through testing [8]. The concept of "fuzzing" was rst introduced by Miller et al. in 1990 [6], and since then, various enhancements have been proposed by integrating different techniques to improve performance and shorten the time needed to identify vulnerabilities.
Existing studies [6-211] primarily focus on adapting conventional fuzzing techniques to speci c IoT scenarios. However, these approaches cannot be easily applied across the diverse spectrum of IoT devices. In [6], the author identi es the monitoring gap in fuzzing embedded systems, as early error detection is di cult due to the absence of hardware protection elements or operating systems. Most fuzzing techniques neglect these factors because it is usually developed for general-purpose systems. Hence, it is crucial to develop specialized fuzzing techniques and tools tailored to tackle the unique challenges presented by embedded systems in IoT environments. These challenges include the absence of hardware protection features or robust operating systems, which complicate early error detection.
There are gaps in automatic con guration as well. For instance, Zhang et al. [10] identi ed gaps indicating the need for more automatic con guration and advanced fuzzing techniques for IoT devices. By highlighting these gaps, we argue that there is a need for a detailed analysis of existing research to identify critical IoT cybersecurity challenges, evaluate the effectiveness of current fuzzing techniques, and propose different methods or frameworks speci cally tailored for IoT environments.

II. BACKGROUND
2.1 Cybersecurity Challenges
IoT technologies include diverse devices such as smartphones, smartwatches, smart homes, vehicles, and agricultural systems. These technologies generate substantial amounts of data, a volume that is anticipated to increase signi cantly in the coming years [3]. The expansive and intricate nature of IoT architecture renders it susceptible to exploitation and cyber-attacks. Hackers can compromise and disrupt IoT networks, impacting their functionality and misusing data [11]. Managing cybersecurity and privacy is essential for IoT platforms, services, and applications.
2.2 Fuzzing Techniques for Vulnerability Discovery
IoT attacks frequently exploit software vulnerabilities, which are security weaknesses that enable hackers to take control of systems. Methods for discovering these vulnerabilities include network discovery tools, machine learning, penetration testing tools, testbeds, and honeypots [12]. Fuzzing is considered the primary technique [6] for this task due to its simplicity, ease of deployment, and thorough veri cation capabilities. Also known as fuzz-testing, fuzzing involves injecting specially crafted inputs into a system, referred to as the System Under Test (SUT), to provoke unexpected behavior. This process helps identify bugs such as memory violations, faulty assertions, improper handling of null values, deadlocks, in nite loops, unde ned behaviors, or inadequate resource management. Compared to other vulnerability discovery methods like code inspection or reverse engineering, fuzzing offers the advantages of automation, scalability, and the ability to run extensively and unattended.
2.3 Fuzzing Process and Importance
The process of fuzzing generally comprises of the following phases:


 Page 4/30
1. Identi cation of the target system, focusing on specifying the SUT's input and output formats.
2. Determining inputs based on identi ed input and output formats aims to minimize the generation of invalid fuzz data while creating accurate and valid inputs. [6].
3. Generating fuzzed data entails altering an initial input (seed) to create new fuzz data. This step is crucial because the success of vulnerability detection is directly related to the quality of the inputs and the choice of seeds [7], [13], [14].
4. Execution of tests with fuzzed data by sending the generated data to the SUT through speci c media (input les, communication packets, analog inputs, etc.) and monitoring the SUT's outputs and behavior for unexpected crashes or outputs that may trigger vulnerabilities [6].
5. Analyzing system behavior involves examining crashes and inputs gathered during the monitoring phase to identify which test cases reveal vulnerabilities or induce abnormal behavior.
6. Issue logging, where the discovered vulnerabilities and the test cases that triggered them are reported for further in-depth analysis and reproduction of crashes [6].
In recent years, fuzzing techniques have garnered considerable attention as an effective method for discovering vulnerabilities and securing complex systems, especially within the fast-evolving realm of the IoT.
2.4 Gaps and Future Directions
The objective is to assess the current state of fuzzing practices and their applications in IoT environments. However, existing research has focused on speci c aspects while overlooking others, resulting in an incomplete picture. While some studies have highlighted real-world IoT attacks, they fail to specify generic and speci c IoT and cybersecurity attacks. Conversely, other studies have presented general-purpose fuzzing frameworks without considering IoTspeci c requirements or future research challenges.
An assessment of selected articles from relevant literature reveals this gap as shown in Table 1. For instance, Eceiza et al. [6] highlighted real-world IoT attacks but did not specify generic and speci c IoT and cybersecurity attacks. In a similar vein, Zhang et al. [10] introduced a general-purpose Binary Fuzzing framework tailored for binary-only fuzzing contexts, without speci c insights into IoT and IoT attacks. On the other hand, Gui et al. [15] offered a comprehensive exploration of IoT, covering both generic and speci c cyber and IoT attacks, yet they did not address future research challenges in this domain.
Likewise, Salazar et al. [16] investigated an ontology focused on network mutation, providing a scienti c basis for research and application methods, but it did not address the speci c needs of IoT. On the other hand, Fortino et al. [17] proposed a new methodology to accelerate security analyses by utilizing conventional security tools such as binary fuzzers on rmware, although they did not speci cally address the security requirements and challenges unique to IoT.

III. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
The PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [18] model provides a structured framework for conducting and reporting systematic reviews and meta-analyses. It guided the selection of relevant papers in this review, ensuring a thorough and transparent synthesis of


 Page 6/30
the literature.
The study utilized a combination of academic databases to source publications from the recent three years (2020 to 2023). The databases included IEEE Xplore, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. The selection of these databases was informed by their distinct characteristics: IEEE Xplore and ScienceDirect are publisher-speci c databases known for their technical and scienti c content, while Web of Science is a comprehensive scholarly database that aggregates publications from a multitude of publishers. This combination allowed for a broad yet focused retrieval of current literature, particularly important as the eld of fuzzing in IoT has seen signi cant advancements post-2020.
A total of 201 articles were initially identi ed: 66 from IEEE Xplore, 10 from ScienceDirect, and 125 from Web of Science. The search employed speci c keywords ('Fuzz' OR 'Fuzzing' AND 'cyber security' AND 'Internet of things' OR 'Framework' OR 'Method') to ensure the inclusion of relevant fuzzing methods or frameworks applied to cybersecurity in IoT. Boolean operators were utilized to re ne the search, aligning it closely with the research objectives.
The selection criteria were designed to ensure the relevance and currency of the information. They were divided into inclusion and exclusion criteria, which comprised:
Inclusion Criteria: Articles must be published in journals, conferences, early access articles, or magazines indexed in IEEE Xplore, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. They must be in English (even if the dataset is not in English) and published within the three years range (2020-2023), focusing on fuzzing, cybersecurity, and IoT approaches, frameworks, or methods.
Exclusion Criteria: Excluded were publications in books, standards, courses, surveys, case studies, or interviews. Also excluded were articles focusing on Industrial IoT (IIoT), power, and communication sectors if not directly related to the primary focus of fuzzing in IoT.
The quality assessment was critical to ensure the selected studies were both relevant and of high quality. From the initial pool of 201 articles, 30 were chosen based on a manual screening of titles and abstracts, with full texts reviewed in selected cases to con rm relevance. This phase was crucial in con rming the applicability of the studies to the research question and ensuring the integrity of the review process.

IV. CLASSIFICATION OF DIFFERENT FUZZING METHODS
Based on our ndings, we introduce a comprehensive framework in this paper for classifying fuzzing methods in IoT in three categories: technique-based, target domain-based, and objective-based classi cation. This framework aims to develop an understanding of various fuzzing approaches and their applications to enhance IoT security.
A)TechniqueBasedClassi cation
The technique-based classi cation categorizes fuzzing methods according to the speci c techniques used in generating and managing test inputs. Understanding these categories is important for grasping the fundamental mechanisms of different fuzzing operations and the types of vulnerabilities they can uncover. The main categories of technique-based classi cation are:
1. Mutation-Based Fuzzing: This technique involves taking valid inputs (seeds) and applying systematic modi cations to produce new test cases. Mutationbased fuzzing is effective for testing a large number of inputs with minimum knowledge about the internal working of the target systems [212].
2.Generation-Based Fuzzing: This type of fuzzing creates inputs from scratch based on speci cations of the input format. Generation-based fuzzing requires complete knowledge about the system to produce accurate and relevant test cases, particularly for complex input structures [213].
3.Evolutionary or Genetic Fuzzing:
This technique applies evolutionary algorithm principles to generate test cases. It starts with an initial set of inputs and uses mechanisms like mutation and recombination guided by a tness function often based on code coverage or the discovery of new paths of execution to evolve inputs that are more likely to uncover vulnerabilities [6].
4. Hybrid Fuzzing: Hybrid fuzzing combines aspects of both static and dynamic analysis, often merging generation-based and mutation-based techniques. It typically begins with generation-based fuzzing to create well-formed inputs and then applies mutation-based fuzzing to explore deeper routes of the software [214].
5. Black-Box Fuzzing: In black-box fuzzing, the fuzzer operates without the source code access or internal SUT logic. Instead, it uses random mutation within prede ned rules to generate those inputs that are valid and the SUT can accept them. The key advantage of black-box fuzzing is its independence from


 Page 7/30
source code, allowing it to target any virtual system. However, assessing code coverage is challenging, and it may not effectively detect errors caused by complex attacks. [6].
6. White-Box Fuzzing: White-box fuzzing uses internal logic information about the source code. The advantage of white-box fuzzing is that it covers the code fully because the fuzzer knows about the source code and internal logic, so it can be assumed which portion of the code executed. However, white-box fuzzing is more complex compared to black-box fuzzing due to the need for full source-code access [6].
7. Grey-Box Fuzzing: Grey-box fuzzing involves gaining partial access of the internal SUT logic [6]. Its execution approach is same as that of black-box fuzzers, but it utilizes less information typically collected through arrangement of the SUT to enhance the coverage and performance [7]. Grey-box fuzzing is widely favored due to its ability to operate with partial source code access, which enhances its e ciency compared to black-box fuzzing.
8. Protocol-based Fuzzing: This technique focuses on testing the network protocol implementation in IoT devices. It uses knowledge about the protocol speci cations to generate inputs, making it particularly relevant for IoT due to their wide range of communication protocols [215].
9. Grammar-based Fuzzing:
This approach uses a formal description of the input format (grammar) to generate test cases. It is especially effective for applications that require structured inputs, such as XML or JSON. Grammar-based fuzzers ensure that inputs conform to grammatical rules while also exploring potentially problematic scenarios [212].
Each of these techniques has unique strengths and is suitable for various fuzzing scenarios. In the context of IoT, the selection of a fuzzing technique may depend on factors such as the computational resources of the device, the complexity of its interfaces, and the speci c security needs of its application domain.
B)TargetDomainBasedClassi cation
Target domain-based classi cation for fuzzing in IoT focuses on categorizing fuzzing techniques based on speci c components, layers, or systems within the Internet of Things ecosystem that they aim to test and secure. This classi cation helps tailor fuzzing approaches to the diverse and specialized components that make up IoT systems, each of which may have unique vulnerabilities and security requirements. The main categories within this classi cation can include:
1. Device Firmware and Hardware: Fuzzing in this domain targets the rmware running on IoT devices or the hardware itself. It aims to uncover vulnerabilities that could allow unauthorized access, rmware manipulation, or physical tampering [216].
2. Communication Protocols: Several IoT devices utilize dedicated protocols like MQTT, CoAP, ZigBee, and Bluetooth for communication. Testing these protocols using fuzzing techniques can uncover implementation weaknesses that may result in man-in-the-middle attacks, data breaches, or device malfunctions [217].
3. IoT Platforms and Backend Systems: This includes the software platforms that manage IoT devices and the backend systems that process IoT data. Fuzzing in this domain focuses on identifying vulnerabilities in these complex systems, which could lead to unauthorized access, data leakage, or denial of service [218].
4. Application Layer: IoT applications that run on devices or interact with devices remotely can also be the target of fuzzing. This includes both the software running on IoT devices and the applications on users' smartphones or computers that control these devices [219], [220].
5. Network and Connectivity: Besides speci c communication protocols, fuzzing can target the broader network infrastructure and connectivity solutions used by IoT devices, including Wi-Fi, cellular networks, and internet gateways. This helps in identifying network-level vulnerabilities that could affect many devices simultaneously [221].
6. Edge and Cloud Computing: Many IoT systems rely on edge or cloud computing for data processing and storage. Fuzzing can be employed on these components to reveal vulnerabilities that may jeopardize data integrity, privacy, or the availability of IoT services [222].
7. APIs and Web Interfaces: APIs (Application Programming Interfaces) and web interfaces are often used for device con guration, management, and integration with other services. Fuzzing these interfaces can reveal security aws in how devices authenticate requests and manage data [223].
By categorizing fuzzing techniques based on the target domain within the IoT ecosystem, researchers and security professionals can develop and apply tailored fuzzing approaches that effectively address the unique challenges and vulnerabilities associated with each component or layer. Each domain presents unique challenges and requires different fuzzing strategies and tools to effectively identify and mitigate potential vulnerabilities.
C)ObjectiveBasedClassi cation
Objective-based classi cation for fuzzing in IoT categorizes fuzzing methods according to the speci c goals or outcomes they aim to achieve within the IoT security landscape. This classi cation helps to align fuzzing efforts with the broader objectives of securing IoT devices and ecosystems, focusing on the types of vulnerabilities or issues that are of most concern. Here are the main categories within objective-based classi cation:
1.Vulnerability Discovery: The primary goal of fuzzing is to systematically uncover software vulnerabilities exploitable by attackers. This includes identifying issues such as buffer over ows, memory leaks, input validation errors, and other software aws that could compromise the security of IoT devices [224].


 Page 8/30
2.Robustness and Stability Testing: Here, the goal is to pinpoint inputs or conditions capable of causing system crashes, hangs, or unpredictable behavior. This aims to enhance the overall stability and reliability of IoT systems, ensuring they can effectively handle unexpected inputs without encountering failures [81].
3.Performance Benchmarking: Some fuzzing efforts aim to evaluate how IoT systems perform under stress or heavy loads, which might include handling large volumes or rapid sequences of inputs. The objective is to identify performance bottlenecks or degradation issues that could affect user experience or system functionality [225].
4.Compliance and Security Policy Veri cation: Fuzzing can also be used to ensure that IoT devices and applications adhere to speci c security standards, protocols, or policies. This includes verifying the correct implementation of cryptographic protocols, authentication mechanisms, and data protection measures [226].
5.Attack Surface Mapping: This objective involves leveraging fuzzing to methodically explore and document the attack surface of an IoT system. This includes identifying exposed interfaces, services, or APIs that might be targeted by attackers. The aim was to gain an in-depth understanding of the system's vulnerabilities and prioritize security efforts accordingly [6].
6.Threat Simulation: In this context, fuzzing is applied to simulate speci c types of cyber-attacks or threat scenarios, evaluating the resilience of IoT systems against real-world threats. This assessment informs the development of more effective defense mechanisms and incident response strategies [227].
By focusing fuzzing efforts on speci c objectives, researchers and practitioners can tailor their approaches to address the most pressing security challenges faced by IoT devices and systems. Each objective requires different fuzzing techniques, tools, and methodologies to effectively identify and mitigate potential risks, ultimately contributing to the development of more secure and resilient IoT ecosystems.

VI. DISCUSSION
This section aims to assess the current state of fuzzing practices and their applications in IoT environments. The current state of fuzzing in IoT is characterized by a rich variety of techniques and tools, each addressing speci c challenges and vulnerabilities. The eld has seen signi cant advancements in terms of coverage, e ciency, and the ability to handle complex IoT environments. However, there are still gaps in terms of comprehensive solutions that can address the full spectrum of IoT security needs possibly by employing AI and machine learning tools. This assessment highlights the need for a comprehensive and holistic approach to fuzzing techniques in IoT environments, addressing diverse aspects of IoT security, including generic and speci c attacks, future research challenges, and the unique requirements of IoT systems. In this section, we classify various fuzzing methodologies applied in the context of IoT and embedded systems. The classi cations include technique-based, target domain-based, and objective-based approaches, each addressing different aspects and challenges of fuzzing in these environments.
6.1 Technique-Based Classi cation
The technique-based classi cation focuses on the development of unique fuzzing techniques for addressing unique challenges of IoT systems. Researchers have proposed a wide range of innovative approaches, including hybrid fuzzing, binary-only fuzzing frameworks, rmware re-hosting methodologies, semantic fuzzing, benchmark suites, hash-based synchronization methods, user-mode emulation, black-box fuzzing, and input message mutation techniques.
One notable trend in this classi cation is the emphasis on improving various aspects of fuzzing, such as performance, exibility, crash triggering, queue quality, and vulnerability detection e ciency. For instance, Eceiza et al. [6] utilized hybrid fuzzing, combining generation-based and mutation-based methods, for embedded systems, employing techniques like full and partial emulation, source code availability, and sanitization.

Researchers have investigated hardware acceleration techniques aimed at enhancing the performance of fuzzing tools. Meisel et al. [231] adapted an opensource hardware accelerated FPGA-based RISC-V fuzzing system, showcasing enhanced performance compared to QEMU-based fuzzing tools such as AFL++.

Coverage-guided fuzzing has become an effective method for enhancing the e ciency and success of fuzzing in IoT systems. By using code coverage as a guiding metric, these fuzzers explore a broader range of code paths and uncover vulnerabilities that traditional fuzzing techniques might overlook.

6.2 Target Domain-Based Classi cation
The target domain-based classi cation encompasses fuzzing methodologies speci cally designed for speci c domains or target systems within the IoT ecosystem. Researchers have developed frameworks like FIRMCORN [15] and FIRM-COV [32], which are tailored for IoT device rmware, focusing on optimized virtual execution and mining real-world vulnerabilities.
Gui et al. [15] developed FIRMCORN, a vulnerable-code search algorithm aimed at identifying fuzzing entry points in IoT device rmware to enhance vulnerability detection e ciency. This algorithm utilizes optimized virtual execution technology to address key issues in IoT rmware fuzzing, including high throughput, accuracy, and emulation stability. Conversely, FIRM-COV, introduced by Kim et al. [32], is the rst high greybox coverage-oriented fuzzer speci cally for IoT rmware. It targets IoT programs and exposes real-world vulnerabilities through optimized process emulation. FIRM-COV addresses IoT fuzzing challenges based on empirical analysis, achieving superior coverage and throughput compared to other fuzzing tools. Another signi cant contribution in this category is ARM-AFL, developed by Fan et al. [235], which is a powerful framework for coverage-guided fuzzing of ARM-based IoT devices. When paired with lwHMCD (lightweight hardware-assisted module containment and deployment), ARM-AFL enables developers to identify vulnerabilities in ARM-based IoT devices before they can be exploited by attackers. Researchers have also explored hybrid frameworks that combine elements of technique-based and target domain-based classi cations. Gui et al. [23] presented FIRMNANO, a rmware fuzzing framework for microcontroller-based IoT devices. FIRMNANO is based on augmented virtual execution and solves three main problems of microcontroller rmware emulation: high throughput, inaccuracy, and instability of emulation. The evaluation result shows that FIRMNANO can execute rmware in the correct manner, and it can be used for the vulnerability mining of real-world rmware.
A key insight from this classi cation is the recognition that different IoT domains and target systems may have distinct requirements and challenges, necessitating the development of specialized fuzzing methodologies. For instance, ARM-based IoT devices may require different approaches compared to microcontroller-based systems, as exempli ed by frameworks like ARM-AFL and FIRMNANO, respectively.
Additionally, the integration of technique-based and target domain-based classi cations, as demonstrated by FIRMNANO, underscores the potential bene ts of merging advanced techniques with domain-speci c factors. This approach leads to more effective and e cient fuzzing methods for IoT systems.

IoT Devices Security and Resilience: The popularity of IoT devices requires effective and e cient methods for assessing the IoT devices security and resilience as disclosed by Shu et al. [29].
Server Software Security: MQTT, most widely used communication protocol for interconnecting the devices with the considerable network restraints, like in IoT [31]. It directly impacts many devices, but the malware protection of the server, i.e., broker implementations is not considered as strong as detected by Pearson et al.
7.4 Challenges in Embedded Systems Fuzzing
Embedded Systems have speci c challenges for fuzzing techniques:
Multi-Architecture Support: Yun et al. [228] identi ed different obstacles, not much study has been done for the Embedded systems fuzzing. Some of them are multi-architecture support, di culties in crash detection, and a smaller number of resources.
Firmware Simulation Di culties: In the rmware vulnerability detection eld, there are different obstacles like closed sources, greater number of hardware dependency which makes effective fuzzing techniques perform very badly [28]. The main issues are di culty in rmware simulation and low throughput as observed by He et al. Firm-AFL solved the throughput issues in AFL to an extent of under rmware system-level simulation by combining Firmadyne and AFL, but further improvement in e ciency is still needed.
Lack of Feedback Mechanism: Due to the lack of feedback [216], effective mutation optimization mechanisms cannot be formed by the existing black box fuzzers to guide the testing processes. Also, by using the available relational communication formats of messages in IoT devices, it is sometimes not possible to apply the strategies of the existing grammar-based fuzzing. In the IoT fuzzing domain, an e cient fuzzing approach is needed with the inference of syntax as revealed by Feng et al.
Lack of Fault Detection Mechanism: When fuzzing is applied to the IoT rmware, there are several challenges that includes: the inability of rmware for the proper execution during the peripheral’s absence, the lack of support to explore the input spaces of multiple peripherals, di culties for the instrumentation and collection of feedback, and the fault detection mechanism absence as uncovered by Situ et al. [30].
Lack of ISA Licensing Costs: In the context of fuzzing, a signi cant challenge arises from the limited observability of tiny IoT processors, which is crucial for effective testing on IoT devices [231]. Meisel et al. proposed addressing the limitations of RISC-V IoT processors to potentially dominate future IoT applications, due to no associated ISA licensing costs.
Protocol Fuzzing Challenges: Ren et al. [233] disclosed that the application of the vulnerability detection techniques namely Zigbee protocol fuzzing is di cult. When dealing with hardware events of low-level, it is a major issue. As Zigbee protocol uses radio channels for communication, most of the current tools for protocol fuzzing need an adequate environment of execution.
7.5 Analysis
The analysis of the limitations and challenges identi ed in the above subsections reveals various issues:
Complexity and Heterogeneity of IoT Devices: Limitations of legacy fuzzing techniques arise due to the complex nature and heterogeneity of IoT devices. Hence, there is a need for customized advanced fuzzers for speci c applications, protocols, and systems.
Scalability and E ciency: There are critical issues of scalability and e ciency especially in blackbox fuzzing and reverse engineering processes. For effective fuzzing, enhancing the dictionary generation, crash veri cation, and scalability is important.
Security Gaps: There are signi cant gaps in IoT devices security assessment, which includes the lack of basic security mechanisms and less focus on security during the development phase.
Embedded Systems: The embedded systems need specialized fuzzing approaches because of their different architecture and resource constraints. To advance fuzzing in this domain, improved simulation techniques, feedback mechanisms, and multi-architecture support are required.
Therefore, this research outlines the need for continuous development of advanced, scalable, and e cient fuzzing techniques according to the various challenges of IoT devices and embedded systems. Solving these limitations will enhance the IoT ecosystem’s security and resilience.
VIII. CONCLUSION & FUTURE WORK
The rising frequency of malicious attacks targeting IoT networks and devices underscores the importance of cybersecurity within the IoT domain. To address this issue, fuzzing has emerged as a prominent technique for vulnerability discovery because of its simplicity, ease of deployment, and effectiveness in uncovering real-world software vulnerabilities. This article presents a systematic exploration of extant fuzz testing methodologies, approaches, and techniques applicable to IoT networks. Through meticulous analysis, we illuminate prevailing gaps and challenges within the realm of fuzzing, thereby offering a comprehensive appraisal of its current state in bolstering IoT security.
8.1 Research Gaps


 Page 17/30
There are several research gaps identi ed in the reviewed literature. The existing fuzzing techniques mostly struggle with the complexity and heterogeneity of IoT devices. Several IoT devices have limited resources and diverse architectures, creating signi cant obstacles for conventional fuzzing methodologies. Furthermore, the lack of a uni ed formalism for explaining fuzzing processes, especially in network-enabled fuzzing, hinders the development of automated test case creation and the execution of complex test scenarios.
8.2 Emerging Trends and Technologies
It pertains to the integration of AI and machine learning in fuzzing techniques. Although signi cant progress has yet to be made, these technologies hold great potential to enhance the e ciency and effectiveness of fuzzing by automating test case generation and improving vulnerability detection. Further, the advancements in re-hosting techniques and the multi-architecture support systems development are important for addressing the limitations of existing fuzzing approaches.
8.3 Unexplored Topics and Methods
There are many unexplored topics and methods which requires further work. For instance, there is a vast area of research in objective-based and target domain-based fuzzing approaches for researchers as there is not much research done. Although, they provide detailed insights into the application of fuzzing techniques within the speci c IoT contexts. In addition, the exploration of linguistic-model-guided fuzzing for non-English languages and the development in the areas of more sophisticated simulation environments for rmware testing are there for future work.
8.4 Methodological Advancements
Methodological Advancements are necessary to overcome the existing limitations. Future research should focus on improving the scalability and e ciency of fuzzing techniques. It includes improving dictionary generation, crash veri cation, and feedback mechanisms in the blackbox fuzzing process. To advance this eld, the standardized benchmarks for fuzzing protocols should be developed and comprehensive frameworks should be created for evaluating fuzzing performance.
8.5 Practical Implications and Applications
The practical implications of improved fuzzing techniques are extensive. The enhanced fuzzing methodologies generate more robust and secure IoT ecosystems which protects against a wide array of cyber threats. By developing the automated, e cient, and scalable fuzzing tools, it will facilitate the practitioners in the widespread use of fuzzing in IoT security assessments. In addition, the creation of standardized protocols and evaluation metrics will set a standard in comparison to the effectiveness of various fuzzing tools and techniques.
8.6 Recommendations for Future Work
The recommendations for future work based on the identi ed gaps, emerging trends, and practical implications are presented as follows:
Objective-Based and Target Domain-Based Fuzzing: Should explore objective-based and target domain-based fuzzing methods to enhance the fuzzing techniques application in diverse IoT contexts.
AI and ML Integration: Enhancing automation of test case generation and improve vulnerability detection of fuzzing processes.
Uni ed Formalisms Development: To create formalized frameworks for the description and automation of network-enabled fuzzing, facilitate the complex test scenarios development.
Linguistic-Model-Guided Fuzzing: The development of fuzzing tools that can perform semantic analysis on non-English languages and address the structural differences in linguistic models.
Standardized Benchmarks: To establish standardized benchmarks and evaluation metrics for fuzzing protocols in order to enable consistent performance assessment.
Enhanced Simulation Environment: Improvement in simulation environment for rmware testing to copy real-world conditions and enhance fuzzing e ciency.
These recommendations will aid in signi cantly enhancing the state of fuzzing methodologies leading to more secure and resilient IoT ecosystems.
